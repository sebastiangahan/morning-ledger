Carney and Trump talk trade for 20 minutes in China, and agree only to keep talking

A day after swapping pork for steel with Beijing, the prime minister sat down with the U.S. president at the APEC summit. Carney called trade with China “much” simpler than trade with the United States.

What happenedPrime Minister Mark Carney met U.S. President Donald Trump for just under 20 minutes on the sidelines of the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation (APEC) summit in Shenzhen, China, according to Carney’s office, The Canadian Press reports. Trump brought a heavyweight team: Trade Representative Jamieson Greer, Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick, Secretary of State Marco Rubio and senior adviser Stephen Miller. Carney was joined by Foreign Affairs Minister Anita Anand and his chief of staff, Marc-André Blanchard. The Canadian readout said only that the two leaders “agreed to continue the work and to stay in close contact.” No deal, deadline or tariff change was announced.

Speaking to reporters, Carney said trade with China is easier than with the U.S., calling the American relationship “much more complex.” He repeated that Canada could turn to Chinese automakers if talks with Washington fail. Separately, CP reports he told Pacific Rim business leaders that Canadians can learn from China’s growth while Ottawa keeps “guardrails” on trade.

The backgroundCanada sends roughly three-quarters of its exports to the U.S. Canada walked away from formal trade talks in August; Washington then put tariffs on billions of dollars of Canadian goods, many at 50%, and Ottawa hit back with its own. Separately, the U.S. declined in July to renew CUSMA, the 2020 free-trade pact with Mexico, which now faces yearly reviews. Carney, a Liberal and former central banker, became prime minister in 2025. Earlier this week he and Chinese President Xi Jinping agreed that China would drop a 25% tariff on Canadian frozen pork and shrimp while Canada eased duties on 32 Chinese steel and aluminum products. Canada also plans a trade mission to China in 2027.

How it is being framed

The Canadian Press (centre). A short, businesslike meeting that kept talks alive, set against Carney’s warmer tone toward Beijing.

CBC (centre-left), headline seen via National Newswatch. Talks are “picking up,” but there is no formal deal on the table.

U.S. side. No White House readout was seen this morning. Yesterday, trade adviser Peter Navarro called Carney’s China pivot “offensive in the extreme.”

Why it matters to you

EconomyU.S. tariffs still weigh on Canadian factory, steel and auto jobs, including in Ontario. A deal would ease prices and layoff risk.

Foreign relationsCanada is trying to win new markets in Asia without angering its biggest customer.

Democracy and ideology lensSupporters say Carney is reducing Canada’s dependence on one powerful neighbour. Critics on the right and among security hawks say praising China’s growth model glosses over its authoritarian system and its record of detaining Canadians. U.S. Democrats say ending the trade war with Canada will be a priority when they take the House in January, so Ottawa has a reason to wait.

Sources: Canadian Press · CP: Carney speech

ConfirmedBudget · Public finances

Budget watchdog: Ottawa will borrow far more than its deficits suggest

What happenedParliamentary Budget Officer Annette Ryan released her analysis of the Nov. 5 federal budget on Tuesday. Her summary: “The government promised to spend less to invest more. This budget spends more, invests more and borrows more.” She projects that measures under Prime Minister Mark Carney add $331.6 billion in costs across three spending plans, and that Ottawa used 96% of its improved fiscal room, mostly from better revenue and higher oil prices, on new spending rather than cutting the deficit. Total borrowing is projected at $765 billion over five years, $476 billion more than the $289-billion combined deficit. Interest on the debt is the fastest-growing item in the budget and is expected to exceed what Ottawa sends provinces for health care.

The backgroundThe Parliamentary Budget Officer (PBO) is an independent office of Parliament that checks the government’s math. A deficit is one year’s gap between spending and revenue; borrowing can be larger because it also pays for loans, investments and maturing debt. Finance Minister François-Philippe Champagne has argued that his budget separates day-to-day “operating” spending, which it aims to balance, from long-term “capital” investment. Ryan said whether that target is met depends on how capital is defined; under international definitions, Ottawa would miss it.

How it is being framed

The Canadian Press (centre). Leads with the borrowing gap and the rising cost of interest.

Government. Champagne: “Confident countries invest.” His spokesperson pointed to falling deficit-to-GDP and debt-to-GDP ratios.

Conservatives. Finance critic Michael Chong says the PBO confirms Ottawa is not on a sustainable track.

Why it matters to you

EconomyInterest costs crowd out money for services and can keep pressure on rates.

PolicyThe fight over “operating” versus “capital” spending will shape the next election debate.

Democracy and ideology lensFiscal conservatives see debt as a burden on young people; Keynesian-leaning economists argue borrowing to build in a trade war is sensible. Independent watchdogs like the PBO matter because they let voters test both claims against the same numbers.

Sources: Canadian Press

ConfirmedJustice · Health

Assisted-dying bill clears key vote, 225 to 89, after Liberals limit debate

What happenedBill C-41 passed second reading in the House of Commons on Tuesday by 225 to 89 and went to the justice committee. It would let people with conditions such as dementia or ALS make an “advance request” for medical assistance in dying (MAID), to be carried out after they lose the capacity to consent; provinces would decide whether to offer it. It would also cancel the planned expansion of MAID to people whose only condition is mental illness, which otherwise takes effect March 17. Justice Minister Sean Fraser limited debate, saying MPs had “ample opportunity” and the deadline requires quick passage.

The backgroundCanada legalized MAID in 2016 after a Supreme Court ruling. Expanding it to mental illness alone has been delayed twice. Second reading is a vote on a bill’s principle; committees then study it and can propose changes.

How it is being framed

The Canadian Press (centre). Focuses on the debate limits and the March deadline.

Conservatives. Support blocking MAID for mental illness but want the bill split so advance requests get more study. Leader Pierre Poilievre said Canada should help people “live their fullest lives”; MP Tamara Jansen called the limits “absolutely shameful.”

Why it matters to you

PolicyFamilies facing dementia could plan end-of-life choices in advance, depending on their province.

Daily lifeThe mental-illness expansion would be stopped before it starts.

Democracy and ideology lensSupporters frame it as personal autonomy; many religious and disability-rights groups worry about pressure on vulnerable people. Cutting debate short on a matter of conscience raises questions about how much deliberation Parliament owes such bills, even with a deadline.

Sources: Canadian Press

Joly to meet Stellantis again as scrutiny grows over Brampton plant sale

What happenedFederal Industry Minister Mélanie Joly will meet Stellantis “once again on Friday,” her office told CTV News, after public scrutiny of the automaker’s sale of its plant in Brampton, Ont. The article available this morning did not give the buyer, the price or how many jobs are affected, so those details are not confirmed here.

The backgroundStellantis, the company behind Chrysler, Jeep and Dodge, has assembled vehicles in Brampton for decades. Ottawa and Ontario have offered billions in subsidies to keep auto production in the province, and U.S. tariffs on Canadian-made cars have pushed automakers to move work south. Selling the plant raises the question of whether those public dollars kept jobs here.

How it is being framed

CTV News (centre). Frames it as the government pressing the company for answers.

Why it matters to you

EconomyBrampton is one of the GTA’s biggest manufacturing hubs; each auto job supports others in parts and services.

Foreign relationsIt is a test of whether U.S. tariffs are pulling auto work out of Ontario.

Democracy and ideology lensThe left asks why taxpayers subsidize companies that leave; free-market voices say subsidies distort business decisions to begin with. Either way, voters want to know what was promised in return for public money.

DevelopingCongress · Lame duck

Outgoing Republican Congress faces a Dec. 11 shutdown deadline

What happenedRepublicans have only weeks left controlling Congress after Democrats won both the House and Senate in the Nov. 3 midterms. In this “lame duck” period, a temporary funding law expires Dec. 11. A short extension into 2027 is more likely than a full spending deal, but even that could be hard to pass and could set off a shutdown, UBS’s Washington analysts wrote on Nov. 13. Other unfinished business includes the annual defence bill, which is caught in disputes over the Iran war and China, a bipartisan Senate deal to speed up permits for energy projects, and a rush to confirm judges and officials.

The backgroundA “lame duck” is the period between an election and when the winners take office; the new Congress is sworn in on Jan. 3. Democrats tied their wins to cost-of-living worries, tariffs and the Iran war. Without a majority in either chamber next year, President Trump will need Democratic votes for any new laws.

How it is being framed

UBS (financial analysts). Expects deadlines to slip into next year; flags shutdown risk.

The Canadian Press (centre). Notes Democrats call ending the trade war with Canada a priority once they hold the House.

Why it matters to you

EconomyA U.S. shutdown can delay border and trade services and rattle markets.

Foreign relationsA Democratic Congress could push back on tariffs against Canada.

Democracy and ideology lensRushing through confirmations and bills after losing an election is legal and common, but critics in both parties say it ignores voters’ verdict. Divided government forces compromise, or gridlock.

Sources: UBS Washington Weekly · Canadian Press

Carney tells Pacific Rim business leaders China’s growth holds lessons for Canada

What happenedAt the APEC business forum in Shenzhen, Carney said Canadians can learn from China’s economic growth, while stressing that Ottawa keeps “guardrails” on trade, according to Canadian Press headlines carried by National Newswatch. The full text was not read this morning, so specifics are not confirmed. The remarks follow an Angus Reid survey finding Canadians open to Chinese companies building electric vehicles in Canada, with worries about jobs and security.

The backgroundShenzhen, next to Hong Kong, grew from a small town into a tech manufacturing hub after China opened it to foreign investment in 1980. China is now the world’s largest maker of EVs and batteries. Canada had a 100% tariff on Chinese EVs until a January deal allowed up to 49,000 a year at 6.1%.

How it is being framed

The Canadian Press (centre), headline. Learning from China, with guardrails.

National Observer (left), column headline. Separately argues the Arctic is the wrong place for Carney’s fossil-fuel plans, a reminder his pro-growth agenda faces criticism from the left too.

Why it matters to you

Foreign relationsOttawa is betting it can trade more with China without importing security risks.

Democracy and ideology lensChina’s growth came under one-party rule, with limited free speech. Holding it up as a model is controversial among human-rights advocates; trade-minded voices argue Canada cannot ignore the world’s second-largest economy.

DevelopingTelecom · Competition

Analysts doubt Quebecor’s $31-billion bid for Telus

What happenedQuebecor, owner of Vidéotron and Freedom Mobile, has proposed a $31-billion cash-and-stock takeover of Telus. Telus shareholders would get $6.35 cash plus 0.17 of a Quebecor share, which Quebecor values at $19.45, against Telus’s $12.14 close on Friday. Telus has not accepted and cites “serious concerns.” RBC’s Drew McReynolds calls a deal a “low probability”; Desjardins puts the odds at 25%. Scotiabank’s Maher Yaghi says it would cut Canada’s national wireless networks from three to two, because Telus shares its network with Bell. Carney would not say whether Ottawa could accept losing a major national player, a Globe and Mail headline reports.

The backgroundCanada’s phone market is dominated by Rogers, Bell and Telus, and prices are among the highest in the rich world. Ottawa approved Rogers’ takeover of Shaw in 2023 on condition that Freedom go to Quebecor to keep a fourth player. Any deal now goes to the Competition Bureau, the CRTC (the telecom regulator) and Industry Minister Mélanie Joly, who promised a “rigorous” review.

How it is being framed

Quebecor. CEO Pierre Karl Péladeau says it keeps critical assets in Canadian hands.

Consumer advocates. Cori Ferguson of the Consumers Council of Canada warns fewer, bigger providers mean “poor service and higher prices.”

Why it matters to you

Daily lifeYour phone bill depends on how many companies compete for you.

Democracy and ideology lensEconomic nationalists value Canadian ownership; competition advocates on left and right say consolidation hurts consumers. Concentrated media and telecom ownership also shapes who controls information.

Sources: Canadian Press · Globe and Mail (headline only)
