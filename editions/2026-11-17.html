Carney and Xi trade pork for steel as Canada leans toward Asia

At the APEC summit in Shenzhen, China drops a 25% tariff on Canadian frozen pork and shrimp; Ottawa eases duties on 32 Chinese steel and aluminum products. A Trump adviser calls Canada’s pivot “offensive.”

What happenedPrime Minister Mark Carney and Chinese President Xi Jinping agreed to lift more trade barriers when they met in Shenzhen during the Asia-Pacific Economic Co-operation (APEC) summit, The Canadian Press reports. China will remove its 25% tariff on Canadian frozen pork cuts and frozen cold-water shrimp, worth about $400 million a year in exports, according to Carney’s spokesperson. Offal (organ meats) stays blocked, the Canadian Meat Advocacy Office noted. In return, Ottawa will extend tariff “remissions”, meaning refunds or waivers, to 32 more Chinese products: 23 steel and nine aluminum. Canola oil access did not expand; Liberal MP Kody Blois, a parliamentary secretary, called it “a much smaller priority” and said “there are areas where we will disagree.” Canadian Meat Council CEO Kyle Larkin said he was “confident with the momentum.”

White House trade adviser Peter Navarro called Carney’s suggestion that Canada could look elsewhere, including to Chinese automakers, if U.S. talks fail “offensive in the extreme.” Canada’s exports to China rose almost 40% in the first nine months of the year, CP reports, and an Angus Reid poll finds Canadians largely open to Chinese electric-vehicle investment, with worries about jobs and security.

The backgroundAPEC is a yearly meeting of 21 Pacific Rim economies, including Canada, the U.S., China and Mexico. Relations with China froze after Canada arrested a Huawei executive in 2018 and China detained two Canadians; they thawed after Carney, a Liberal and former central banker who became prime minister in 2025, struck a deal in January. That deal cut Canada’s tariff on Chinese EVs from 100% to 6.1% for up to 49,000 vehicles a year, and 16,211 have arrived since March. The U.S. hit many Canadian goods with 50% tariffs this summer, which is why Ottawa is courting other markets.

How it is being framed

The Canadian Press (centre). A transactional win for farmers and fishers, with canola still unresolved.

National Post (right), via Yahoo. Its China coverage today centres on security: Commons bug sweeps for MPs on the foreign affairs committee (see In brief).

U.S. administration. Navarro’s comments frame it as Canada playing Washington against Beijing.

Why it matters to you

EconomyPork and seafood producers regain a big market; domestic steelmakers may face more Chinese competition.

Foreign relationsGetting closer to Beijing could complicate talks with Washington, Canada’s largest customer by far.

Democracy and ideology lensFree-traders on the centre-right and centre-left welcome new markets. Security hawks across parties warn about depending on an authoritarian state that has detained Canadians and is accused of interference here; labour voices worry about cheaper Chinese steel and cars. Toronto’s incident at the Chinese consulate (see In brief) shows why the debate is not only about money.

Sources: Canadian Press · CP: exports · Angus Reid

DevelopingTrade · North America

Carney and Sheinbaum meet in China as the U.S. keeps North American trade on a short leash

What happenedCarney met Mexican President Claudia Sheinbaum on the sidelines of APEC in Shenzhen. Carney pointed to “many opportunities for further co-operation,” including with the U.S., and said 120 initiatives from a September 2025 action plan are complete, with two-way trade up 18% year over year. Sheinbaum called it an “extraordinary relationship” and said they discussed continental trade and a possible three-country deal, CP reports. Back home, Trade Minister Dominic LeBlanc will chair a meeting of the Canada–U.S. economic advisory committee on Thursday, CTV reports. A Nanos survey finds support for a U.S. deal that still includes tariffs has fallen to 33.3%, and former U.S. ambassadors told CP a deal is possible even if some tariffs stay.

The backgroundCUSMA (called USMCA in the U.S.) is the 2020 free-trade pact among Canada, the U.S. and Mexico. In July the Trump administration declined to renew it, which triggers yearly reviews for up to a decade; without agreement it expires. U.S. Trade Representative Jamieson Greer wants limited interim deals by year-end. After talks broke down this summer, Trump put 50% tariffs on many Canadian goods and Canada retaliated; Mexico has not. Trade lawyer Andrea Van Vugt said “it’s not just Ambassador Greer,” pointing to Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick’s role; Mexico’s former negotiator Kenneth Smith Ramos called the talks “a game of chicken.”

How it is being framed

The Canadian Press (centre). Two middle powers navigating “Trump’s tumult” together.

CTV / Nanos (centre). Public patience is thin: most Canadians would reject a deal that keeps tariffs.

Right-leaning outlets. I did not see their take on the Sheinbaum meeting this morning.

Why it matters to you

EconomyCUSMA’s future decides whether GTA auto and parts plants can sell into the U.S. tariff-free.

Foreign relationsA Canada–Mexico front could strengthen both in talks, or annoy Washington.

Democracy and ideology lensTrade policy is set mostly by executives, not legislatures, in all three countries. Some argue that lets leaders move fast in a crisis; others say deals this big deserve more parliamentary and congressional debate.

Ontario moves on intimate-image abuse as Catholic teachers vote to strike if needed

What happenedOn the legislature’s first day back, Premier Doug Ford’s government tabled a bill creating a faster legal route for victims to get intimate images deleted, CP24 reports. The same day, Ontario’s Catholic teachers voted 98% in favour of a strike mandate, CP24 reports. Toronto’s new city council meets for the first time on Wednesday and Thursday.

Unverified. I have not seen the bill’s number, the penalties it sets, or when a strike could legally begin.

The backgroundIntimate-image abuse means sharing sexual photos or videos without consent, including AI-made “deepfakes.” It is already a crime under the federal Criminal Code, but getting images taken down through the courts can be slow; provinces can create civil remedies. Ford leads the Progressive Conservatives, who hold a majority. A strike mandate is a union vote that authorizes, but does not start, a strike; it is mainly leverage at the bargaining table. The Ontario English Catholic Teachers’ Association represents teachers in Catholic schools, which are publicly funded in Ontario.

How it is being framed

CP24 (centre). Both covered straight: a victims’ rights bill and a lopsided strike vote.

Other outlets. I did not see right- or left-leaning takes this morning.

Why it matters to you

Daily lifeFamilies with kids in Catholic schools could face disruption if talks fail.

PolicyA civil takedown route could help victims faster than criminal cases.

Democracy and ideology lensTeacher bargaining is a long-running left-right fight in Ontario: unions say wages and class sizes are at stake; the government stresses taxpayer costs. On the image bill, privacy and victims’ advocates largely agree; civil-liberties groups usually watch how far takedown orders reach online.

DisputedPrivacy · Bill C-22

Meta says it would defy Ottawa rather than weaken encryption

What happenedMeta, owner of Facebook, Instagram and WhatsApp, says it would refuse a ministerial order under Canada’s proposed lawful-access law if it required weakening encryption, The Globe and Mail reports. Bloomberg reports Meta, Google and Apple are lobbying to rewrite Bill C-22 before it passes. Both headlines only.

The background“Lawful access” laws let police and spy agencies obtain data from tech companies with legal authorization. End-to-end encryption means only the sender and recipient can read a message, not even the company. Police say it lets criminals hide; companies and privacy experts say any “back door” for police can also be used by hackers and hostile states.

Unverified. I have not read the bill’s text or the government’s response.

Daily lifeYour private messages’ security could change.

Democracy and ideology lensThis splits along security versus civil-liberties lines more than left versus right. A foreign company openly saying it would refuse a Canadian law also raises a sovereignty question.

Sources: Globe and Mail · Bloomberg
