DevelopingLead story · Trade · Canada–U.S.

Washington’s trade chief says the U.S. and Canada are “fairly close” to a deal

Jamieson Greer’s comment is the most upbeat signal from the Trump administration in months. No terms have been published, and Canadian columnists across the spectrum are warning against expecting too much.

What happenedU.S. Trade Representative Jamieson Greer said on Sunday that the two countries are “fairly close” to agreeing on a trade deal, CTV News reported. The trade representative is the U.S. cabinet-level official who negotiates trade agreements for the president. Only CTV’s summary of the interview could be read this morning; the full article did not load, so the details of what is on the table, and whether tariffs on steel, aluminum, autos and lumber would come off, are not confirmed here.

The background

Since early 2025, President Donald Trump has placed tariffs, which are taxes paid on imported goods, on many Canadian products, and Canada answered with counter-tariffs on hundreds of U.S. goods. Prime Minister Mark Carney, the Liberal leader and a former central banker, has said repeatedly that a deal must be good for Canada rather than fast. Talks have stopped and restarted several times. The North American free trade pact, known in Canada as CUSMA, is also under its scheduled review, which gives Washington extra leverage. Canada sends roughly three-quarters of its exports to the U.S., so tariffs hit jobs in Ontario’s auto and steel towns first.

How it is being framed

CTV News (centre). Headlines Greer’s optimism directly, as a possible turning point.

Toronto Sun (right), Lorrie Goldstein column, headline only. “Carney oversold what Canada can realistically achieve on U.S. tariffs.”

Toronto Star (centre-left), column, headline only. “Mark Carney navigates the art of no deal with Donald Trump,” a play on Trump’s book title.

Hill Times (non-partisan, Ottawa trade paper), opinion, headline only. “What the Trump thumping may mean for Canada,” on whether Republican losses in this month’s U.S. midterms change the calculus.

Why it matters to you

EconomyForeign relationsDaily life

Democracy and ideology lensTrade deals are negotiated in private by the executive branch and reach legislatures mostly as take-it-or-leave-it packages. “Fairly close” is a negotiating signal, not a text. Free-market voices tend to judge a deal by whether tariffs fall; labour and nationalist voices, left and right, by what Canada gives up on dairy, digital rules or defence spending.

Canada

Federal politics and foreign policy
ConfirmedPolls · Federal

Two polls, one picture: the Liberals still lead comfortably, but one tracker shows the gap closing

What happenedLiaison Strategies’ weekly tracker, released Monday, puts the Liberals at 41%, the Conservatives at 32%, the NDP at 15% and the Bloc Québécois at 6%. That is a 9-point lead, down from 15 a week earlier. Carney’s approval is 62%, down 2. The poll surveyed 1,000 people by automated phone calls from Nov. 8 to 21 (margin of error ±3.1 points). Pollster David Valentin said the Liberals “have lost four points after a month at 44 or 45 per cent,” but added: “One update isn’t enough to call a new trend.” Abacus Data, polling 2,000 adults online from Nov. 13 to 18 (±2.1), has the Liberals at 45% and Conservatives at 35%, and says “the vote has barely moved” since the Nov. 5 budget.

The background

The Liberals won the 2025 election under Carney. The Conservatives, led by Pierre Poilievre, are the official Opposition; the NDP is the left-of-centre party and the Bloc runs only in Quebec. A poll’s margin of error describes random sampling noise, so a 9-point and a 10-point lead are statistically the same story. Different methods, phone robocalls versus online panels, also produce different numbers. Abacus found the cost of living is the top issue for 67%, and the budget projects a $62.3-billion deficit.

How it is being framed

Liaison Strategies (pollster). Leads with the narrowing, while warning it may be noise.

Abacus Data (pollster). Leads with stability after the budget.

Toronto Star (centre-left), opinion, headline only. “Why Mark Carney needs Pierre Poilievre to stay.” Globe and Mail (centre-right), Robyn Urback column, headline only: donors “have started giving up” on Poilievre.

Why it matters to you

Policy

Democracy and ideology lensA minority-sized lead can still mean a majority government under first-past-the-post, where seats, not vote share, decide power. Polls shape coverage and donations, which can become self-fulfilling. Treat a single week’s move with caution.

Sources: Liaison Strategies · Abacus Data

ConfirmedElections · Alberta

Alberta plans to require proof of citizenship to vote, likely starting with the 2027 election

What happenedPremier Danielle Smith told a virtual town hall for her United Conservative Party that her government will likely bring in a law next spring requiring voters to prove they are citizens, The Canadian Press reports. Accepted ID would include a passport, a birth certificate or an updated driver’s licence with a citizenship marker. She told Albertans to get their documents in order before the provincial election on Oct. 18, 2027, and said last month’s referendum vote gave her a mandate.

The background

The United Conservative Party (UCP) is Alberta’s governing right-of-centre party. Canadian elections already require voters to be citizens, but today voters swear to it and show ID proving identity and address, not citizenship. Elections Canada and Elections Alberta, the independent agencies that run votes, report very few cases of ineligible voting. Similar “proof of citizenship” rules are a live fight in the United States.

How it is being framed

The Canadian Press (centre). Presents Smith’s plan alongside critics who say it makes voting harder and opens the door to bias, and the election agencies’ data on rare fraud.

Premier Smith (via CP). Frames it as carrying out a voter-approved mandate and protecting election integrity.

Why it matters to you

PolicyDaily life

Democracy and ideology lensThe core trade-off: access versus security. Supporters, mostly on the right, say confidence in results is worth extra paperwork. Critics, mostly on the left, note that citizens without a passport, often poorer, older, rural or Indigenous voters, could be turned away to stop fraud that the data says is rare.

Sources: The Canadian Press

ConfirmedCity hall · New term

Chow begins her second term with a reshaped council: seven newcomers, a new speaker and two ex-MPs

What happenedMayor Olivia Chow laid out her vision for the 2026–2030 term at the new council’s first meeting last week, The Canadian Press reported on Nov. 18. Her platform promises include renter protections, capped transit fares, a bigger school nutrition program and more affordable housing. Seven new councillors join, including former Liberal MPs Nate Erskine-Smith and Shaun Chen. Frances Nunziata, council’s speaker for 16 years, lost her seat, so the chair goes to someone new. Transit chair Jamaal Myers was also defeated, and Gord Perks has retired as housing chair.

The background

Toronto’s mayor, elected in October, has “strong mayor” powers from Doug Ford’s Progressive Conservative government: she can pass some items, such as the budget, with just a third of council’s support. Chow, a former NDP MP, leads a council where progressives hold more sway than under past mayors. The speaker runs meetings; committee chairs steer housing and transit files. This story is from last week and is included because the new term’s agenda is just taking shape.

How it is being framed

The Canadian Press, on CP24 (centre). A who’s-in, who’s-out piece focused on turnover, with no direct quotes.

Other outlets. Not read this morning, so left and right framing is not reported.

DisputedCongress · Oversight

The White House warns incoming Democrats that investigating Trump could become a crime; Democrats say he is “hiding something”

What happenedAfter Democrats won control of Congress in this month’s midterms, the White House warned that their planned investigations of President Trump could cross into criminal conduct, the Associated Press reported in its live coverage (updated Nov. 12). Rep. Robert Garcia, the likely incoming chair of the House Oversight Committee, said Trump “is clearly hiding something.” The current Republican chair, James Comer, said the committee is “very independent.” In the same coverage, AP called Mississippi’s Senate race for Democrat Scott Colom, who beat Sen. Cindy Hyde-Smith by 4,669 votes, about half a point; she conceded.

The background

The House Oversight Committee can subpoena documents and witnesses, and is the main tool a party uses to investigate a president of the other party. The new Congress takes over on Jan. 3. This item is about 11 days old; it is here because the fight over oversight will shape Washington through 2027. The White House’s claim of “criminality” has not been backed with a specific law, which is why this story is marked Disputed.

How it is being framed

Associated Press (centre). Presents both sides’ claims in a rolling live blog without endorsing either.

Other outlets. No partisan U.S. coverage was read this morning.

Why it matters to you

Foreign relationsPolicy

Democracy and ideology lensChecks and balances depend on Congress being able to investigate the executive. Describing oversight as possibly criminal is unusual; supporters of the president see the probes as partisan revenge. For Canada, a Congress fighting the White House can mean slower or tougher votes on trade.

DevelopingChina · Trade strategy

After the Trump–Xi–Putin summit, Canadian commentators argue over how close to get to China

What happenedWith Washington, Beijing and Moscow meeting at the top without Canada (see Sunday’s edition), columnists in Canada’s two national papers are debating how far Ottawa should lean toward China to cushion U.S. tariffs. No new government decision was announced; this story tracks the debate, from headlines only.

The background

Canada and China have been at odds since 2018, over arrests of citizens, tariffs on Chinese electric vehicles and Chinese duties on Canadian canola. Carney’s government has reopened some doors, including talks on Chinese investment in the auto sector. Businesses want new markets; security agencies warn about technology and influence.

How it is being framed

National Post (right), opinion, headline only. “If Carney tries to play China against the U.S., Canada will lose.”

Globe and Mail (centre-right), opinion, headline only. “On trade with China, where do you draw the line? And how?”

Why it matters to you

Foreign relationsEconomy

Democracy and ideology lensTrading with an authoritarian state raises the values-versus-prosperity question. Realists say a middle power must hedge; others argue that dependence on Beijing is riskier than dependence on Washington.

Sources: National Post, Globe and Mail (headlines via National Newswatch)

DevelopingPrivacy · Senate

Big tech and police make final pitches as the Senate weighs Bill C-22 on access to your data

What happenedTech companies and law enforcement made their last arguments as the Senate considers the future of a “data access” bill, CBC News reported Monday (headline only; CBC could not be read).

The background

“Lawful access” bills set rules for when police and spy agencies can make phone and internet companies hand over subscriber data or build in ways to intercept communications. The Senate is Parliament’s appointed upper house; it rarely kills bills but can amend them. Police say they need faster access for child exploitation and organized crime cases; tech firms and privacy advocates warn about security weaknesses and overreach.

Why it matters to you

PolicyDaily life

Democracy and ideology lensPrivacy versus public safety splits both left and right: civil libertarians on both sides oppose broad access, while law-and-order voices favour it.

Sources: CBC News (headline via National Newswatch)
