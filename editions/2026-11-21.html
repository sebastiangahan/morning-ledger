Ontario Liberals crown a new leader today, their fourth try since 2018 at a formula to beat Doug Ford

Results from a ranked ballot of about 55,000 members are due later today. Lee Fairclough and Navdeep Bains are seen as the favourites; whoever wins inherits a party with 14 seats and a long climb.

What happenedThe Ontario Liberal Party will reveal its new leader later today after weeks of voting and months of campaigning, The Canadian Press reports. About 55,000 members were eligible to vote using a ranked ballot, meaning they listed candidates in order of preference and the lowest finisher’s votes are redistributed until someone has a majority. Four candidates are running: Lee Fairclough, a member of the provincial legislature and former hospital president; Navdeep Bains, a former federal Liberal cabinet minister now working as a Rogers Communications executive; Stephanie Bowman, a member of the legislature and former bank executive; and Dylan Marando, a former political staffer. CP describes Fairclough and Bains as the favourites. No vote counts had been released when this edition was written, so the winner is not reported here.

The backgroundThe Liberals governed Ontario for 15 years until 2018, when Doug Ford’s Progressive Conservatives (the provincial centre-right party) swept them out and Premier Kathleen Wynne resigned. Since then the party has struggled to find a lasting leader. Steven Del Duca won in 2020 and quit two years later after failing to win his own seat. Bonnie Crombie, the former mayor of Mississauga, led from late 2023 until early this year, when she stepped aside after a weak result in a leadership review at the party’s annual meeting. Ford’s PCs hold a large majority, and the NDP (the left-of-centre New Democrats) is the Official Opposition, the party that leads the questioning of the government in the legislature. One practical issue: of the four, only Fairclough and Bowman already sit in the legislature. A leader without a seat cannot face Ford directly in Question Period, the daily session where opposition members question the government.

How it is being framed

The Canadian Press (centre). Plays it straight: a long contest, two front-runners, and a party on its third leadership race since 2018.

Toronto Star (centre-left), Adam Radwanski column, headline only. Asks whether the Liberals “have finally figured out how to take on Doug Ford.”

Conservative-leaning outlets. No coverage of the race from right-leaning outlets was seen this morning.

Why it matters to you

PolicyThe new leader will be one of the main voices against Ford on housing, health care, highways and transit before the next provincial election.

Daily lifeA stronger opposition tends to mean more scrutiny of decisions on things like hospital wait times and the cost of the Highway 413 and Ontario Place projects.

Democracy and ideology lensOntario’s centre is crowded: Ford’s PCs have moved toward working-class suburban voters, while the NDP and Liberals split the centre-left vote. Ranked ballots reward a candidate who is many members’ second choice, not just the most loved. Whether a big-name outsider (Bains) or a sitting MPP (Fairclough, Bowman) wins is also a test of whether parties value name recognition or day-to-day presence in the legislature.

Poilievre says he won’t quit even if the Conservatives lose the Brantford byelection

What happenedConservative Leader Pierre Poilievre says he will not resign if his party loses an upcoming federal byelection in Brantford, Ont., according to a Toronto Star headline this morning. The full article could not be read, so the date of the vote, the riding’s full name and Poilievre’s exact words are not reported here. The comment follows a week of columns questioning his future, including the Globe and Mail’s “Donors have started giving up on Pierre Poilievre” and the Star’s “Even his own staff is abandoning him.”

The backgroundPoilievre has led the Conservatives, Canada’s main right-of-centre party, since 2022. The party lost the 2025 election to Mark Carney’s Liberals. A byelection is a vote in one riding (electoral district) to fill a seat that has become empty between general elections. Brantford, southwest of Hamilton, has recently leaned Conservative, which is why a loss there would be read as a warning sign. Nanos Research tracking released Nov. 17 had the Liberals at 45.4% and the Conservatives at 28.6%.

How it is being framed

Toronto Star (centre-left), headline only. Leads with Poilievre ruling out resignation, which presents the byelection as a test of his leadership.

Hill Times (non-partisan, Parliament Hill trade paper), opinion, headline only. “Byelections are usually a blip, until they force a party reckoning.”

The Guardian (centre-left, U.K.), headline only. Turns the question around: Carney “is so popular he can get away with a lot. Will it last if the Trump spell breaks?”

Conservative-leaning outlets. No defence of Poilievre from right-leaning outlets was seen this morning. That gap is noted, not judged.

Why it matters to you

PolicyAn opposition busy with its own leadership puts less pressure on the government’s trade, China and budget choices.

Democracy and ideology lensLabelled “Disputed” because whether Poilievre is in trouble is an argument, not a settled fact: the evidence so far is columns and one poll. Conservatives can formally review their leader at party conventions; leaders often survive bad byelections. A credible opposition matters in any parliamentary system, because it gives voters a real alternative.

Sources: Toronto Star (headline) · Hill Times (headline) · The Guardian (headline) · Nanos Research

DevelopingTrade · Canada–U.S.

A U.S. senator says Canada should take an interim tariff deal and let Congress write the long-term one

What happenedA United States senator says Canada should reach an interim tariff agreement with the Trump administration and leave Congress to “legislate” a long-term deal, CBC News reported Friday, according to its headline. The senator’s name and party were not in the headline seen. It follows a federal minister’s comment this week that an interim deal would “kickstart” wider talks. Commentary is split on whether Ottawa is being patient or giving ground: a CBC political panel titled its Friday episode “Has Canada Blinked?”

The backgroundAn interim deal is a smaller, temporary agreement, such as lowering some tariffs, while a full deal is negotiated. CUSMA (the Canada–United States–Mexico Agreement) is the North American free-trade pact that replaced NAFTA in 2020. Tariffs are taxes on imports; U.S. tariffs on Canadian steel, aluminum and autos have hit Ontario hardest. The senator’s idea matters because Congress, which Democrats take over on Jan. 3, has the constitutional power over trade but has let presidents set tariffs using emergency powers. A deal written into law would be harder for a future president to tear up.

How it is being framed

CBC News (centre-left), headline seen via National Newswatch. Frames an interim deal as a bridge to something more durable written by Congress.

Toronto Star (centre-left), Susan Delacourt column, headline only. “Mark Carney navigates the art of no deal with Donald Trump.”

Good Talk panel (Mansbridge, Hébert, Anderson), title only. Asks whether Canada has “blinked.”

Why it matters to you

EconomyAny tariff relief would reach Ontario steel, aluminum and auto workers first.

Foreign relationsOttawa may be betting on the incoming Congress rather than the White House for a lasting settlement.

Democracy and ideology lensThe debate is partly about who should set trade rules: an elected legislature through law, or a president through emergency orders. Supporters of free trade on the right and labour voices on the left both want protection from sudden tariffs, but disagree on what Canada should give up to get it.

Sources: CBC News (headline) · Toronto Star (headline) · Good Talk (title)

ConfirmedCity hall · New term

Chow starts her second term with a $1.1-billion hole and a new speaker in the chair

What happenedMayor Olivia Chow, sworn in for a second term after winning more than 50% of the vote, told the first meeting of the 2026–2030 council that she will deliver on a mandate to make Toronto “more caring, more affordable, and safer,” CP24 reports. She listed affordable housing, protecting renters from bad landlords, expanding school lunches, reliable low-cost TTC service, traffic congestion and grocery costs as priorities. The city faces a $1.1-billion shortfall next year, and Chow wants a “structured financial solution” from Ontario and Ottawa: “We cannot keep going back to Queens Park and Ottawa year after year.” Council elected Ausma Malik (Spadina–Fort York) as speaker, the only nominee, replacing Frances Nunziata after 16 years; Shelley Carroll is deputy speaker.

The backgroundChow is a former NDP member of Parliament who first won the mayoralty in a 2023 by-election. Toronto, like all Ontario cities, cannot run an operating deficit, so a shortfall must be closed with tax increases, cuts or help from other governments. Seven councillors are new, including two former federal Liberal MPs, Nate Erskine-Smith and Shaun Chen. The speaker runs council meetings and rules on debate, a role that matters on a council with strong opinions on every side.

How it is being framed

CP24 (centre). A fresh start with turnover in key roles; quotes councillors Mike Layton (“a good group”), Amber Morley (“Pace yourself”) and Curra Stikuts, the youngest member, who says she will speak for millennials.

Why it matters to you

Daily lifeTTC fares, rent rules and school meals all run through this council.

EconomyHow the $1.1-billion gap is closed will show up in your property tax or rent.

Democracy and ideology lensChow’s pitch is that cities carry provincial and federal costs, such as refugee housing and transit, without matching revenue. Fiscal conservatives answer that the city should find savings first. Both views agree the current year-by-year bailouts are a poor way to run a city of three million.

Sources: CP24 · City of Toronto

DevelopingCongress · Lame duck

A defeated Republican Congress has three weeks to keep the government open and pick new leaders

What happenedRepublicans still control Congress until Jan. 3, but after Democrats flipped both chambers in the Nov. 3 midterms, the outgoing majority faces a crowded “lame-duck” session, the Associated Press reported earlier this month. Government funding runs out Dec. 11. House Speaker Mike Johnson is stepping aside, with Steve Scalise and Jim Jordan expected to compete to lead House Republicans; Senate Majority Leader John Thune is expected to stay on as minority leader. Republican Rep. Thomas Massie has pushed a resolution to impeach Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth. Trump defended Hegseth: “He’s guilty of two things, basically winning two wars.” House Democratic leader Hakeem Jeffries said: “I’m a hard yes on the Hegseth resolution.” The AP report is from Nov. 9; no newer congressional story was read this morning.

The backgroundA “lame duck” is a session after an election and before the newly elected members take office. The Speaker is the most powerful member of the House of Representatives and controls which bills get a vote. To impeach means to formally accuse an official; it takes a simple majority in the House, while removal needs two-thirds of the Senate. Stephen Miller, a hardline immigration adviser, has become Trump’s chief of staff.

How it is being framed

Associated Press (centre). A party in retreat with unfinished business; quotes Democrat Rep.-elect Daniel Biss saying voters have “had enough of Donald Trump.”

Republican voices quoted by AP. Trump backs his defence secretary as a winner.

Why it matters to you

Foreign relationsA distracted Washington makes a quick Canada trade deal less likely before January.

EconomyA U.S. shutdown can slow border services and rattle markets.

Democracy and ideology lensPeaceful handovers of power and congressional checks on the executive are core to American democracy. A Republican lawmaker leading an impeachment push against his own party’s cabinet member shows how much the election loss has loosened party discipline.

Sources: AP via Newser

DevelopingTech policy · Data centres

Trump threatens a “tremendous tax” on U.S. companies that build data centres abroad

What happenedPresident Donald Trump has threatened U.S. companies that build data centres outside the country with a “tremendous tax,” Global News reported Friday, according to its technology page. The article itself was not read, so which companies, which countries and how the tax would work are not known from this morning’s research. Treat it as a threat, not a policy: no bill or executive order was seen.

The backgroundData centres are large buildings full of computers that run cloud services and train artificial-intelligence systems. They need huge amounts of electricity and cooling, which is why Canada, with cheap hydro power and a cold climate, has courted them. Many planned Canadian projects are backed by U.S. tech giants. Ottawa has also been pushing “sovereign cloud,” meaning data stored and controlled in Canada, partly to reduce reliance on American firms.

How it is being framed

Global News (centre), headline only. Frames it as a threat aimed at U.S. firms, filed under U.S. news.

Why it matters to you

EconomyA U.S. penalty could chill investment in data centres in Quebec, Ontario and Alberta.

Foreign relationsIt would add a tech front to the existing tariff fight.

Democracy and ideology lens“America First” economic nationalism treats investment abroad as jobs lost at home; free-market conservatives and most economists see such taxes as raising costs for everyone. For Canada, it strengthens the case made by both left and right for building domestic computing capacity.

Sources: Global News (headline)

DevelopingTelecom · Competition

Quebecor reportedly bids $31 billion for Telus, and analysts expect a bidding war

What happenedQuebecor, owner of Freedom Mobile and Vidéotron, has made a multibillion-dollar offer for Telus, Global News reported Thursday; a Global News video cites $31 billion. Analysts expect a bidding war. On Thursday the Globe and Mail reported, citing unnamed sources, that Ottawa is open to a three-player telecom market, in a story about Quebecor criticizing a possible BCE takeover of Telus. Neither Telus nor the government’s response was read this morning.

The backgroundCanada’s three national carriers are Bell (owned by BCE), Rogers and Telus. Quebecor became the fourth national player after buying Freedom in 2023 as a condition of the Rogers–Shaw merger. Any deal needs approval from the Competition Bureau (which guards against monopolies), the CRTC (the telecom regulator) and the federal industry minister.

How it is being framed

Global News (centre), headline and video. A takeover fight with more bidders likely.

Globe and Mail (centre), headline only. Focuses on Ottawa’s openness to consolidation.

Why it matters to you

Daily lifeCanadians already pay some of the world’s highest phone bills; fewer competitors usually means higher prices.

PolicyThe decision will show whether Ottawa still wants four national carriers.

Democracy and ideology lensSupporters of consolidation argue that bigger firms can afford rural networks and stand up to U.S. rivals; consumer advocates on the left and pro-competition voices on the right warn of less choice.

Sources: Global News (headline, video) · Globe and Mail (headline)
