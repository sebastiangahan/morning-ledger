DevelopingLead story · Constitution · Quebec

Supreme Court rules today on Quebec’s religious-symbols law, and on how far provinces can override Charter rights

The ruling on Bill 21 could set the limits of the notwithstanding clause, which four provinces have used since 2019. Premiers are watching.

What happenedThe Supreme Court of Canada is scheduled to release its decision this morning on Quebec’s secularism law, known as Bill 21, The Canadian Press and Global News report. The court will decide whether Quebec could use the Charter’s notwithstanding clause to shield the law from challenges. The case drew 61 parties, including 51 interveners, which the court says is a record. The judges heard four days of arguments last March. Premiers across the country say they are awaiting the result. The ruling may already be out by the time you read this.

The background

Bill 21 was passed on June 16, 2019, by the Coalition Avenir Québec (CAQ) government of then-premier François Legault, who called it a “moderate” way to settle a long debate. It bars public-sector workers in positions of authority, such as police officers, judges and public-school teachers, from wearing religious symbols like a hijab, kippah or turban at work, and requires people receiving government services to show their faces. The Charter of Rights and Freedoms protects freedom of religion and equality, but Section 33, the notwithstanding clause, lets a legislature override some of those rights for renewable five-year periods. Quebec used it pre-emptively, before any court ruled. Since 2019, Quebec, Ontario, Saskatchewan and Alberta have all invoked the clause. Lower courts upheld most of the law. The federal government intervened to ask the court to put limits on how provinces use the clause.

Why it matters to you

PolicyDaily life

Democracy and ideology lensThis is a clash between two democratic ideas: elected legislatures having the final word, and courts protecting minority rights from majorities. Supporters of Bill 21 see a neutral state; opponents see a law that mostly affects Muslim women, Sikh men and Jewish people. If the court limits the clause, provinces lose a tool Ontario has also used; if it does not, overrides may become routine.

“A vote for Dave Carrol is a vote for the Liberals”: Conservative flyers target an ex-Conservative in a tight Ontario byelection

What happenedDave Carrol, an independent running in the Brantford–Brant South federal byelection on Dec. 7, received Conservative flyers warning voters that “a vote for Dave Carrol is a vote for the Liberals,” the National Post reports. Carrol is a former president of the local Conservative riding association; the party revoked his membership when he entered the race. “It’s a weird thing when you get a letter telling you that you shouldn’t trust you,” he said. Conservative spokesperson Sarah Fischer said the party authorized the literature: “All rules were followed, and the information is accurate.”

The background

A byelection fills a single seat in the House of Commons between general elections. Conservative Leader Pierre Poilievre named outgoing Brantford mayor Kevin Davis as the candidate from the stage of a Sept. 20 rally, skipping the usual local nomination vote, which is part of why some local Conservatives left. Polls cited at the time suggested a “dead heat” between the Conservatives and Prime Minister Mark Carney’s Liberals. An independent who splits the conservative vote could decide the result. Davis has not responded to interview requests.

DevelopingAutos · Climate rules · Jobs

Ford and Unifor ask Ottawa to waive carmakers’ emissions penalties, calling them an “own goal”

What happenedOntario Premier Doug Ford and Lana Payne, president of Unifor, Canada’s largest private-sector union, jointly urged Ottawa on Thursday to exempt automakers from penalties for missing tailpipe-emission standards, The Canadian Press reports. They say the rules force manufacturers to buy credits from companies that do not build cars in Canada, an “own goal” that helps President Trump’s aim of moving auto jobs south. They want the exemption tied to “a firm commitment that automotive production will be preserved in Canada,” and a “full reversal” of the sale of Stellantis’s Brampton assembly plant.

The background

Tailpipe standards cap the average emissions of the cars a company sells. Firms that miss the target can buy credits from those that beat it, which mostly means electric-car makers. Environment Canada data show Ford and GM bought more than 9 million credits since 2021 while Tesla sold about 12 million. Brian Kingston of the Canadian Vehicle Manufacturers’ Association estimates U.S. tariffs have cost Canadian makers about $8 billion. Carney said last month he would keep the 2026 standards until at least 2029 instead of tightening them. Transport produces 28% of Canada’s emissions.

How it is being framed

National Post, John Ivison column (right). Carney “can have an auto industry or his tailpipe climate rules, but probably not both”; cites Ontario minister Vic Fedeli saying the rules cost Stellantis $330 million a year.

Federal Conservatives. Industry critic Adam Chambers says carbon pricing and emissions rules cost Canadian jobs.

After a Toronto musician is killed in a home invasion, Ford tells Ontarians to fight back “by any means necessary”

What happenedPremier Doug Ford said Thursday that people should protect their families “by any means necessary” during home invasions, The Canadian Press reports. “I encourage people to use whatever tool they have to fight back, not hide, but fight back,” he said, adding that he owns a gun and would use it. He has told his attorney general and solicitor general to examine laws “right up to the edge” of a castle law. His comments followed the death of Michael Eckert, 39, a pedal-steel guitarist shot in an overnight home invasion near Poplar Plains Road and St. Clair Avenue West on Wednesday. Police are seeking a suspect.

The background

A “castle law”, common in U.S. states, presumes that people may use force, even deadly force, against an intruder at home. In Canada, self-defence is set out in the Criminal Code, which allows force that is “reasonable in the circumstances”. Criminal law is federal, so Ontario cannot rewrite it alone; it can lobby Ottawa and shape how its prosecutors handle cases. Attorney General Doug Downey said self-defence rules already exist and will be reviewed. Most guns in Canada require a licence, and owning one for self-protection is generally not allowed.

DisputedIran war · Gulf · Oil

Drone strike in Kuwait kills two Italian technicians as the U.S.–Iran war spreads across the Gulf

What happenedDrones hit a Kuwaiti military facility on Thursday, killing two Italian technicians, CBS News reports; Kuwait says four people were injured. Iran’s embassy in Rome denied responsibility. European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen said, “We must step up pressure on Iran.” Houthi rebels in Yemen, who are backed by Iran, also struck Saudi targets, including a refinery in Jazan where a Nepali worker was killed.

The background

The United States has been fighting a war with Iran this year, and the conflict has spread to shipping in and around the Strait of Hormuz, the narrow channel that carries a large share of the world’s oil. The International Maritime Organization counts 141 attacks on commercial ships near Hormuz since February, and the data firm Kpler says weekly transits fell from 217 to 147 between Nov. 1 and Nov. 23. Oil prices rose slightly after a drop of about 20%. Israel’s defence minister, Israel Katz, alleged on Tuesday that Iran is developing chemical weapons.

DevelopingRussia–Ukraine · NATO · Black Sea

From under 50 incidents to as many as 500: the Black Sea war is edging toward NATO

What happenedNATO counted fewer than 50 incidents in the Black Sea in 2025, and “four to five hundred” this year, NATO spokesman Col. Martin O’Donnell told CBS News. The International Transport Workers’ Federation says at least 40 sailors have been killed there this year. Ukraine said it struck a Russian warship near Libya, and Poland called a Russian missile strike on its territory an “intentional provocation.”

The background

The Black Sea borders Ukraine and Russia, as well as NATO members Turkey, Romania and Bulgaria, so attacks on ships and ports there can hit allied territory or citizens. Article 5 of the NATO treaty says an attack on one member is an attack on all; it has been formally invoked only once, after Sept. 11, 2001. Canada is a NATO member and leads the alliance’s multinational brigade in Latvia. Separately, a BBC analysis of leaked records counts at least 490,334 Russian soldiers dead, while the independent outlet Mediazona estimates about 700,000. Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov said only the Defence Ministry can disclose casualty figures.

How it is being framed

CBS News (centre-left). A front that could “pull the U.S. into the conflict.”

Fox News (right), headline only. Leads with the higher figure: “Putin’s war death toll reportedly tops 700,000.”

Fox News (right). Reports Britain contacting thousands of former troops and spending an extra £470 million on security at 44 defence sites.

Foreign relationsPolicy

AI, platforms and the people who govern them
ConfirmedCloud · Outage · Government

Microsoft declares a “Code Red” after an outage that hit governments, including Canada’s

What happenedMicrosoft has launched an internal “Code Red” after a Nov. 12–13 outage knocked out Exchange Online, Outlook, Teams and its Copilot AI assistant, Global News reports. Governments in Canada, the U.S., Australia and Japan were affected. Microsoft blamed network strain, not AI or a cyberattack, and CEO Satya Nadella apologized. A memo seen by Reuters quotes executive Charles Lamanna saying the failed component had “roughly ten years’ worth of acceptable failures.”

The background

Much of government and business email and video calling runs on a few “cloud” providers that rent out computing from giant data centres. That is cheap and convenient, but a single failure can stop work across countries. Canada has been discussing “sovereign cloud”, meaning computing kept under Canadian control. Analyst Ritesh Kotak described a Code Red as an “all hands on deck situation.”

Why it matters to you

EconomyDaily life

Democracy and ideology lensRelying on a foreign company for core government work trades control for convenience, a question that matters more as Canada–U.S. ties strain.
