“If the U.S. doesn’t want to do the deal, we’re busy”: Carney ends his Asia trip with a warning

After a short meeting with Trump, the prime minister said a “very good” trade arrangement is possible but not there yet. He leaves with a Philippines trade deal and better access to China, but nothing new from Washington.

What happenedSpeaking by video to the New York Times DealBook Summit from Shenzhen, China, Prime Minister Mark Carney said a “very good trade arrangement” with the United States is possible but has not been reached. “We would like to do the deal. But if the U.S. doesn’t want to do the deal, we’re busy,” he said, adding that a deal “should have been done six months ago,” The Canadian Press reports. It followed his roughly 20-minute meeting with President Donald Trump at the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation (APEC) summit, which he called a “nice discussion.” His office said the two discussed “a mutually beneficial trade agreement” that includes protections for the French language and a viable auto sector.

The “busy” refers to the rest of the trip. In Manila, Carney and Philippine President Ferdinand Marcos Jr. concluded free-trade talks, with signing expected next year; the deal targets Canadian farm, forestry, mining and energy exports. In Shenzhen, he and Chinese President Xi Jinping eased restrictions on more goods: more Canadian pork into China, more Chinese steel and aluminum into Canada. He then flew to Dublin for the European Political Community summit, where Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy briefed about a dozen leaders on talks with Russia.

The backgroundCarney, a Liberal and former governor of the Bank of Canada, became prime minister in 2025. Roughly three-quarters of Canada’s exports go to the U.S. Talks broke down in August and Washington imposed tariffs, many at 50%, on Canadian goods; Ottawa retaliated. The 2020 trade pact between Canada, the U.S. and Mexico (CUSMA) was not renewed in July and now faces yearly reviews. “Diversification” is Carney’s term for selling more to countries other than the U.S.

How it is being framed

The Canadian Press (centre). Leads with Carney’s “we’re busy” line and lists six takeaways, from pork to French-language protection.

CBC (centre-left), headline seen via National Newswatch. Same quote as its headline; it reported earlier this week that talks between top officials were “ramping up.”

Conservatives. Trade critic Stephanie Kusie said engaging China is “always concerning” and called diversification a “distraction” from failing to land a U.S. deal.

U.S. side. No White House statement on the meeting was seen this morning.

Why it matters to you

EconomyTariffs still hit Ontario steel, auto and factory jobs. A U.S. deal is the single biggest lever for prices and layoffs here.

Foreign relationsCanada is signalling it has options, a negotiating tactic that only works if Washington believes it.

Democracy and ideology lensFree-trade liberals and many business groups back finding new markets. Economic nationalists on the right and security hawks worry deals with China trade away leverage and values. Carney is also betting on time: Democrats take control of the U.S. Congress in January and have called ending the trade war with Canada a priority.

Sources: Canadian Press: deal possible · CP: six takeaways · CBC (headline only)

Is China safe for Canadians? Anand points to the travel advisory, and won’t say more

What happenedForeign Affairs Minister Anita Anand, asked in Shenzhen whether China is safe for Canadians to visit, said Global Affairs Canada keeps its travel advisories accurate: “We are ensuring that those are always accurate.” She would not give a yes or no. She said she raised human rights, press freedom, transnational repression and the treatment of Canadians with her Chinese counterpart, Wang Yi. Canada’s advisory for China tells visitors to “exercise a high degree of caution,” the second-lowest of four levels, and warns that China can hold foreigners without charge for up to 37 days. Carney and Xi also announced renewed funding for university exchanges.

The backgroundIn 2018, after Canada arrested Huawei executive Meng Wanzhou on a U.S. extradition request, China detained Michael Kovrig and Michael Spavor for more than 1,000 days, a case widely seen as retaliation. “Transnational repression” means a government harassing or intimidating people abroad, including diaspora communities in Canada. Jeff Nankivell, head of the Asia Pacific Foundation of Canada, a non-partisan think tank, said China carries “unusual risks,” with business disputes now the biggest, and called a repeat of the “Two Michaels” case unlikely but more likely than before 2018.

How it is being framed

The Canadian Press (centre). Frames the question as the human cost of warmer ties with Beijing.

Conservatives. Kusie’s line that engaging China is “always concerning” reflects the party’s wider skepticism.

Why it matters to you

Foreign relationsMore trade means more Canadians travelling and working in China, with real legal risks.

Daily lifeStudents and business travellers should read the advisory before going.

Democracy and ideology lensDemocracies face a trade-off: engagement can open markets and channels for raising rights abuses, but it can also make governments quieter about them. How plainly a minister answers “is it safe?” is a small test of that balance.

Sources: Canadian Press

Ottawa to review where Chinese EVs can drive, echoing Ford’s “spy car” warnings

What happenedTransport Minister Steve MacKinnon said Ottawa will review where Chinese-made electric vehicles may be driven, Global News reported Wednesday. Asked whether they could be kept off military and government sites, he said that would be part of a “cross-governmental review” with Public Safety and other departments, and that the government is “always on the lookout for risks” from connected vehicles. Transport Canada is still certifying some Chinese automakers; BYD and Chery want to sell here.

The backgroundIn January, Carney cut the tariff on Chinese EVs from 100% to 6.1% for up to 49,000 vehicles a year. Modern “connected” cars send location, camera and driving data over the internet, which is why several allies are cautious: Germany recently barred some Chinese vehicles from secure military and government sites, according to Der Spiegel, and the U.K., Poland and Czechia have full or partial bans on bases. U.S. Ambassador Pete Hoekstra has said Canadians driving Chinese cars into the U.S. would be stopped at the border. Ontario Premier Doug Ford, who leads the Progressive Conservatives and whose province is the heart of Canada’s auto industry, has repeatedly called them “spy cars.”

How it is being framed

Global News (centre). Leads with the security review and the allied precedents.

Ontario government. Ford’s “spy cars” line frames the issue as both security and protecting Ontario auto jobs.

Why it matters to you

Daily lifeCheaper EVs may come with limits on where you can drive them, and a problem at the U.S. border.

EconomyOntario autoworkers see low-cost Chinese cars as competition; buyers see savings.

Democracy and ideology lensPrivacy advocates on the left and security hawks on the right often agree that foreign-controlled data collection is a risk. Free-traders reply that consumers should choose, and that Western-made cars collect plenty of data too.

Sources: Global News

Zelenskyy briefs Europe on Russia talks as Canada pledges ammunition and winter gear

What happenedAt the European Political Community summit in Dublin, Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy briefed Carney and about a dozen European leaders on his talks with Russia, CP reports; Carney said Canada would stand by its NATO allies. Separately, a CP headline says Ottawa will supply Ukraine with ammunition, winter clothing and food. Quantities and dollar figures were not in what was read this morning, so they are not reported here.

The backgroundRussia launched its full-scale invasion of Ukraine in February 2022. The European Political Community is a forum of about 47 European countries, wider than the EU, created after the invasion; Canada attends as a guest. NATO is the Western military alliance; Canada is a founding member. Winter aid matters because Russian strikes on power plants leave Ukrainians without heat.

How it is being framed

The Canadian Press (centre). Treats Dublin as a reminder of Canada’s European ties at the end of an Asia-focused trip.

Why it matters to you

Foreign relationsHow the war ends shapes Europe’s security and NATO spending, which Canada is raising.

Democracy and ideology lensSupporters see aid to Ukraine as defending a democracy against invasion. Some on the populist right in North America and Europe question the cost; some on the left push for faster peace talks. The terms of any deal will test whether borders can be changed by force.

Meta and Ottawa were close to a deal to bring news back, report says

What happenedPolitico, citing an unnamed official, reported that Meta and the federal government were close to a deal before Canada–U.S. trade talks collapsed in August. Under it, Meta would resume carrying news in Canada, pursue commercial deals with publishers and fund two positions at the Canadian Journalism Collective, in exchange for an exemption from the Online News Act. Culture Minister Marc Miller called the talks confidential: “Meta could always put news back online. They are the ones who removed it.” Meta declined to comment, CP reports.

The backgroundThe Online News Act (2023) requires big platforms to pay Canadian news outlets when their content is shared. Meta responded by blocking news on Facebook and Instagram in Canada. Google instead agreed to pay $100 million a year and got an exemption. This is separate from Meta’s fight with Ottawa over Bill C-22, the lawful-access bill covered earlier this week.

How it is being framed

News Media Canada (publishers). Paul Deegan: any exemption only with “fair compensation… just as Google has.”

Canadian Association of Broadcasters. Kevin Desjardins: a “deal is never a deal until it’s done.”

Why it matters to you

Daily lifeNews links could return to your Facebook and Instagram feeds.

PolicyTests whether Ottawa trades digital rules for U.S. goodwill.

Democracy and ideology lensSupporters of the law say platforms profit from journalism and should pay; critics on the right and some tech-policy experts call it a link tax that backfired. Since the ban, studies have found less news on social feeds, which matters for how voters get information.

Sources: Canadian Press · Politico (as cited by CP)
