DevelopingLead story · Ontario auto sector

Stellantis sells its Brampton plant to a defence firm, and Ottawa says the company broke its word

The fight is now about whether billions of dollars in public subsidy promises can actually be enforced.

What happenedStellantis, the multinational that owns Jeep, Dodge and Chrysler, confirmed Monday that it has signed a deal to sell its Brampton assembly plant to Roshel, a Canadian company that builds armoured vehicles for police and militaries. The plant has made cars since 1986 but has built nothing since December 2023, when the last Dodge Chargers and Challengers came off the line.

The backgroundThe plant was supposed to be retooled to build the Jeep Compass. Stellantis paused that work in early 2025, after U.S. President Donald Trump put tariffs on vehicles made outside the United States, and later moved the Compass to Illinois. That matters because in 2023 the federal and Ontario governments promised Stellantis up to $15 billion over 10 years, mainly to secure an electric-vehicle battery factory in Windsor. In return the company agreed to keep its Ontario operations, Brampton included, running through 2035.

What every outlet agrees onAbout 2,200 workers are on layoff. The sale price has not been made public. Ottawa opened a formal contract dispute with Stellantis in October 2025. Industry Minister Mélanie Joly has called a meeting for Thursday with the company, the province and Unifor, the union that represents the workers and is Canada’s largest private-sector union.

How it is being framed

Globe, CBC, Global (centre). These lead with Prime Minister Mark Carney, a Liberal, calling the sale “a betrayal” and “completely outrageous,” and promising to “fully enforce our rights” to recover public money.

Toronto Sun, National Post (right). These lead with Conservative Leader Pierre Poilievre, who heads the Official Opposition. He says Canada is at a “breaking point,” wants Joly fired, and wants Ottawa to sue now rather than keep threatening to. The Sun notes that Canadian auto output has fallen from 2.2 million vehicles in 2016 to about 1.2 million.

CP24 (local). Roshel’s chief executive says the company “is not going to even consider” taking responsibility for the laid-off workers, though it will favour Brampton residents when it hires. Unifor says the deal lets Stellantis walk away from its commitments.

The complication. CTV reports, citing unnamed sources, that Ottawa was closely involved in the sale talks and did not block the deal. That sits awkwardly beside the “betrayal” language. Nobody has confirmed it on the record.

Why it matters to you

EconomyAn assembly plant is the centre of a web of suppliers. Flavio Volpe, who leads the auto-parts industry association, says Brampton once supported 10,000 to 12,000 supplier jobs across the Toronto region. Armoured vehicles are a far smaller business than mass-market cars.

PolicyThe $15 billion was a performance incentive, paid as the company delivers, so how much has actually gone out is unclear. If Ottawa cannot claw money back, every future subsidy deal becomes harder to defend.

Foreign relationsThe work left for Illinois under U.S. tariff pressure, and Trump has threatened a 50% tariff on Canadian autos from Jan. 1.

Democracy and ideology lensPaying companies to build here is called industrial policy. In this case a Liberal federal government and a Progressive Conservative provincial one both signed on. Free-market conservatives argue governments should not pick winners. The left argues public money should buy enforceable guarantees or an ownership stake. This case tests whether the contracts had teeth.

Canada

Federal politics and foreign policy
ConfirmedForeign policy

Carney heads to Asia to sell trade, with a warning for Xi in his pocket

What happenedAfter this morning’s Remembrance Day ceremony in Ottawa, the Prime Minister flies to Dublin, then on to three summits: one in the Philippines, the Francophonie (the club of French-speaking nations) in Cambodia, and APEC, the forum of 21 Pacific economies, in Shenzhen, China, next week. He said Tuesday he will talk with Chinese President Xi Jinping about expanding trade and about “non-interference in our affairs by China.”

The backgroundRoughly three-quarters of what Canada sells abroad goes to the United States. Since U.S. tariffs began, Carney has made finding other customers his central economic goal, and he has named China and India as targets. The difficulty is that a Canadian public inquiry concluded China meddled in the 2019 and 2021 federal elections and called it the most active foreign interferer in Canadian democracy. Relations collapsed under the previous prime minister, Justin Trudeau. Carney visited Beijing in January to begin repairing them.

The other sideChina’s foreign ministry said today it has “never had any interest in interfering in Canada’s internal affairs,” which is its standing position.

How it is being framed

CBC, Canadian Press. A bridge-building tour to reduce dependence on the U.S. An Angus Reid poll finds Canadians growing more open to trade with China and India.

Globe and Mail. Stresses the security warning to Xi. CBC separately reported that Beijing blocked the Canadian embassy’s Tiananmen Square commemorations for two years.

Right-leaning outlets. Question courting Beijing at all while interference files remain open. I found less coverage here than elsewhere this morning.

Why it matters to you

EconomyEvery new market lowers the cost of the next American tariff threat. Farmers and energy producers have the most to gain from Asian buyers.

Foreign relationsCloser ties with Beijing could irritate Washington in the middle of trade talks.

Democracy and ideology lensThis is the old trade-off between an interests-first foreign policy (do business with whoever buys) and a values-first one (hold back from governments that undermine democracy). The test is whether interference is raised in the room or only at the press conference.

Sources: CP24/AFP · CBC · Global · PMO itinerary

DevelopingCanada–U.S. trade

A trade deal “by year-end”? The clock that matters is Jan. 1

Where things standCanada and the U.S. are negotiating to ease the tariffs, or import taxes, that Washington has placed on Canadian goods. Carney suspended the talks in August over American demands he said threatened Canadian sovereignty and cultural protections. He and Trump spoke on Nov. 1, and “more intensive” negotiations have resumed. The U.S. ambassador to Canada, Pete Hoekstra, predicts a deal by Dec. 31.

The backgroundMost Canada–U.S. trade runs under CUSMA, the 2020 agreement that replaced NAFTA and keeps most goods tariff-free. Trump has gone around it using national-security powers. U.S. tariffs on Canadian steel, aluminum and copper run as high as 50%, and autos face 25% with exemptions for American-made content. In July Washington declined to renew CUSMA for another 16 years, so the pact now comes up for review every year.

The midterm questionDemocrats, who are generally cooler on tariffs, just won Congress. Both Hoekstra and Canada’s trade minister, Dominic LeBlanc, say that changes little, because tariffs are set by the president. No terms of any deal have been published.

Why it matters to you

EconomyA 50% auto tariff would land hardest on Ontario: Windsor, Oshawa, Alliston, Ingersoll and the parts plants around Toronto.

Daily lifeTariffs, and Canada’s counter-tariffs, feed into the price of cars, appliances and canned goods.

Democracy and ideology lensTariffs used to be a cause of the protectionist left. Trump has made them a tool of the populist right, while many traditional conservatives still favour free trade. In the U.S. the power sits with one office, the presidency, and that concentration is itself being argued over in American courts.

Sources: Radio-Canada · Reuters · Hashtag Investing summary

Ontario and Toronto

Queen’s Park and City Hall
ConfirmedLocal democracy

Ford government hand-picks the heads of eight regional governments

What happenedMunicipal Affairs Minister Rob Flack on Tuesday named the chairs of Durham, Halton, Niagara, Peel, Waterloo, York, Muskoka and Simcoe. They include Vaughan Mayor Steven Del Duca for York, Pickering Mayor Kevin Ashe for Durham, and Scott Aitchison, a federal Conservative MP who is leaving Ottawa, for Muskoka.

The backgroundOutside Toronto, much of Ontario has two layers of local government. Your city or town handles things like parks and local roads. A regional government above it runs police, water, public health and major transit for several municipalities at once, and its chair is the most powerful local official in the area. Until now chairs were either elected by residents or chosen by regional councillors. A law passed earlier this year by Premier Doug Ford’s Progressive Conservative government lets the minister appoint them instead and gives them “strong chair” powers over budgets and decisions.

How it is being framed

Government. Experienced local leaders who will keep regions “in sync” with the province and speed up housing and growth.

Opposition and critics. A transfer of power away from local voters and councils to the provincial government at Queen’s Park.

Worth noting. The picks cross party lines. Del Duca led the Ontario Liberals against Ford in the 2022 election. Chairs of the large regions earned more than $225,000 last year.

Why it matters to you

PolicyRegional chairs steer budgets for police, transit, water and roads for millions of people around Toronto.

Daily lifeIf you live in the 905, the person with the most say over your regional taxes was not chosen by you or by your council.

Democracy and ideology lensThis is centralisation in the name of efficiency. It is unusual for a conservative government, since conservatism usually favours local control. The democratic cost is accountability: an appointed chair answers to the minister, not to voters.

Sources: CP24 · CTV · The Trillium

DevelopingCity Hall

“There’s no 2.0”: Ford closes the door on a new funding deal for Toronto

What happenedPremier Ford said last week that Toronto will not get a second version of the “New Deal,” citing a $16.9-billion provincial deficit. “We’re in tough times,” he said. He left room for targeted help on crime and subway safety.

The backgroundToronto spends more than it can raise. In 2023, shortly after Olivia Chow was first elected mayor, the city and province signed a three-year rescue: $1.2 billion in operating money, plus the province taking over the Gardiner Expressway and Don Valley Parkway, two highways the city could no longer afford to maintain. That deal runs out this year. Chow, who comes from the NDP and governs from the left, was re-elected on Oct. 26 over Councillor Brad Bradford, her main challenger from the right, and had hoped for a successor deal. She now says she will pursue priorities with the province one at a time.

How it is being framed

Province. Toronto has received $9 billion with federal help and must manage its own budget.

Greens and Liberals. Without a deal, cities face double-digit property-tax increases and worsening homelessness. They also say Ford overspent on construction and has little left to share.

City. CBC reports a hiring pause over “financial pressures.” One outlet puts next year’s gap at $1.1 billion. I could not confirm that figure from city documents.

Why it matters to you

Daily lifeChow promised tax increases near the rate of inflation. Without provincial money, the choices are higher property taxes, cuts to the TTC and other services, or delayed repairs.

PolicyThe new council writes the 2027 budget this winter. It has seven new members, including former federal Liberal minister Nate Erskine-Smith and a returning Mike Layton.

Democracy and ideology lensCanadian cities have no standing in the Constitution. They are “creatures of the province,” which can change their powers at will, and they rely mostly on property tax. Fiscal conservatives say that forces discipline. Progressives say it leaves cities with big-city responsibilities and small-town tools.

Sources: Global News · CP24 · CBC · Global (election)

United States

One week after the midterms
DevelopingCongress

Democrats take both houses of Congress. Republicans have seven weeks left to use theirs

The backgroundAmericans vote for Congress every two years. The votes halfway through a president’s four-year term are called midterms, and they usually punish the party in the White House. Congress has two halves: the 435-seat House of Representatives and the 100-seat Senate. Republicans have controlled both since January 2025, which let President Trump govern with little resistance. The winners do not take their seats until January. The weeks in between are called the “lame duck” session, when the outgoing Congress can still pass laws.

The countDemocrats have won at least 254 House seats, up from 214, with about 17 races still uncalled. They will hold at least 54 Senate seats after winning in Texas, for the first time since 1988, and Kansas. Mississippi and Alaska are still counting. New York’s Hakeem Jeffries is expected to become Speaker, the House’s presiding officer.

What is happening nowGovernment funding runs out Dec. 11. Without a new spending law, non-essential federal services shut down. The House voted 211–204 to shelve an attempt, written by a Republican, to impeach Defence Secretary Pete Hegseth over the war with Iran. Trump replaced his chief of staff with hard-line adviser Stephen Miller, said Republicans lost because he was not on the ballot, and ordered the Justice Department to look into Democratic “impeachment threats.”

How it is being framed

CBS, NBC (centre-left). A landslide and a rebuke of the Iran war. Democrats plan investigations into the Trump family’s business dealings, immigration enforcement and the Epstein files.

Fox News (right). A “brutal” lame duck. Conservatives want a Republican-only bill with defence money and $1 trillion in cuts before January. Some admit there is not enough time.

Fiscal Times (non-partisan). The likely outcome is a short-term funding patch, leaving the real fight to the new Congress.

Why it matters to Canada

Foreign relationsJeffries lists ending Trump’s tariffs as a priority, but tariffs sit with the president. Do not expect quick relief.

EconomyA U.S. shutdown slows border processing and rattles markets. By Trump’s own count, U.S. stocks lost about $3 trillion the day after the vote.

Democracy and ideology lensDivided government restores a check that has been missing for two years: the power to investigate, to subpoena and to control budgets. Two things to watch are a president pointing prosecutors at political opponents, and a defeated majority passing major laws on its way out. The second is legal and long practised, but contested.

Sources: CBS News · Fox News · Fiscal Times · BBC/SCMP via 10 Things

DisputedIran · Oil

Trump says the Iran war is ending “fairly soon.” Tehran calls the talks “fake news”

What happenedAfter meeting Qatar’s prime minister, who is acting as a go-between, Trump said further U.S. strikes on Iran are “quite unlikely.” Oil fell to a two-month low on the news, then climbed back today to about US$92.50 a barrel after new attacks on commercial ships in the Strait of Hormuz. A U.S. naval blockade of Iran remains in place.

The backgroundThe United States and Israel have been striking Iran this year, aiming at its nuclear and missile programs. Iran sits beside the Strait of Hormuz, a narrow channel that carries roughly a fifth of the world’s oil, so fighting there moves prices everywhere. Oil began 2026 near $60 a barrel and peaked near $130 in the spring. The sticking points are that Washington wants Iran’s enriched nuclear material removed from the country, and Tehran will not guarantee free passage through the strait.

What is disputedWhether talks exist at all. Iranian officials deny it. This week Iran showed off an underground missile base and dropped its self-imposed 2,000 km limit on missile range. The U.S. moved aircraft out of a second British base over Iranian threats, and France blames Iran for a cyberattack that warmed supermarket freezers nationwide. Iran has not accepted responsibility.

Why it matters to you

Daily lifeOil is the main reason for higher pump prices and for the Bank of Canada’s first interest-rate increase since 2023. The Bank raises rates to cool inflation, and that makes variable mortgages and renewals cost more.

EconomyCanada is pulled both ways. Expensive oil lifts Alberta’s revenues and hurts Ontario households. The country lost 4,700 jobs in October and unemployment is 6.7%.

DevelopingUkraine

Russia and Ukraine sit down in Abu Dhabi for the first U.S.-brokered talks in nine months

What happenedTuesday’s session lasted about four hours and continues today. U.S. Secretary of State Marco Rubio called it productive, and Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy called it constructive. They discussed prisoner exchanges and security guarantees for Ukraine.

The backgroundRussia launched its full-scale invasion of Ukraine in February 2022, and the war is now in its fifth year. The gap between the two sides has not moved. Moscow wants Ukraine to give up territory and abandon its bid to join NATO, the Western military alliance. Kyiv wants a ceasefire first and refuses to hand over land. European governments want a seat at the table and Russia is resisting. Both sides are still hitting each other’s infrastructure with long-range strikes.

Why it matters to Canada

Foreign relationsZelenskyy says allies are discussing guarantees modelled on NATO’s promise that an attack on one is an attack on all. Canada is one of those allies and has one of the world’s largest Ukrainian communities, so a deal could come with a request for Canadian money or troops.

Democracy and ideology lensThe same week, Russia’s last independent newspaper, Novaya Gazeta, closed its Moscow newsroom after losing its licence, its lease and its bank access. Six of its journalists have been murdered since 1993. The Kremlin called the closure normal industry turnover.

Sources: PBS, Reuters, NYT, DW via 10 Things · Viewstorys roundup

DevelopingMarkets · AI

The company behind Claude starts selling itself to Wall Street

Anthropic could raise about US$64 billion, one of the largest stock-market debuts ever.

Disclosure: this newsletter is produced with help from Claude, an Anthropic product.

What happenedAnthropic began pitching big investors on a Nasdaq listing under the ticker ANT, at $70 to $78 a share. It plans to sell about 825 million shares. Regulators have not yet cleared the filing, so the price and date can still change.

The backgroundAn IPO (initial public offering) is when a private company first sells shares to the public. AI companies have spent huge sums on computer chips and data centres. OpenAI, its main rival, reportedly hit about $10 billion in revenue last quarter but is still losing large amounts of money.

Why it matters to you

EconomyCanadian pension funds and index funds could end up owning a piece. A strong debut would signal that investors still believe in AI spending. A weak one could drag down tech stocks broadly.

The debateSupporters say public markets bring more scrutiny to powerful AI firms. Sceptics say the valuations assume profits that no AI lab has shown yet.

ConfirmedCanada · AI

Ottawa now owns part of a Toronto AI company, and a new tech unicorn appears

What happenedCanada and Toronto-based Cohere signed an international AI safety pledge alongside Anthropic, OpenAI and others. That follows Ottawa’s plan to invest up to $1.6 billion in Cohere as part of a $7-billion funding round, which values the company at $36 billion. Separately, Toronto’s Blue J, which uses AI to answer tax questions, passed a $1-billion valuation, making it Canada’s newest “unicorn.”

The backgroundOttawa calls this “sovereign AI”: keeping important AI built and controlled in Canada instead of relying on U.S. firms.

The debateSupporters say it keeps talent and data at home. Critics, including fiscal conservatives, question taxpayers taking a stake in a private, money-losing company.

In brief

Smaller stories worth knowing

Ottawa won’t defend its environmental review law

The Impact Assessment Act lets the federal government review big projects such as mines and pipelines for environmental harm. Alberta says it intrudes on provincial powers, and its top court again found the law unconstitutional last month. The Toronto Star reports Ottawa will not defend it at the Supreme Court. That is a win for provinces and resource projects and a loss for federal oversight. Developing

“Mr. Carney is not my boss”

Quebec’s new premier, Paul St-Pierre Plamondon, leads the Parti Québécois, which wants Quebec to become its own country. He wrote to 59 newly elected U.S. officials offering direct trade ties. Carney says Canada needs a single negotiator. Expect more of this: the PQ’s aim is for Quebec to act like a state on the world stage. Confirmed

Liberals lead by 16 points

A Nanos poll has the governing Liberals at 44%, the Conservatives at 28% and the NDP at 12%. Carney leads Poilievre 54% to 20% as preferred prime minister. Separately, the National Post reports that the B.C. Conservatives’ provincial election win is lifting federal Conservative morale.

Israel’s parliament turns on Netanyahu’s legacy

After an election, opposition leader Gadi Eisenkot has six weeks to assemble a governing coalition. His bloc has 48 seats, Benjamin Netanyahu’s has 53, and 61 are needed. Opposition bills would open an inquiry into the Oct. 7, 2023 attacks and repeal Netanyahu’s overhaul of the courts.

Arctic drilling back on the table

Ottawa is considering lifting a decade-old ban on offshore oil and gas exploration in the Arctic, the Star reports. An Ipsos poll in the National Post finds 81% of Canadians support new pipelines and export terminals.
